# Live animations: rules

The rules for every live three.js animation on the company websites (`/animations/*`). They come from the reviews of the first piece, the pharma line on the Rosepetal website; its figures appear below as worked examples. Never ship a piece that breaks a rule here. When a review raises something new, write it down as a rule in the piece's review log before fixing it.

## What a piece is

- **A live scene, not a film.** The machine, the line, the products and the software are in the page, drawn live. No rendered frame sequences, no batches of stills.
- **The original storyboard is the content checklist, not the look.** Every beat, figure and claim of the original stays, explained in a serious, well-designed way. Its styling, pacing and devices don't. A redesign never drops a panel's content to simplify.
- **Preserve markings that explain the quotation** (11-26). When a quotation uses colour to identify equipment or specifies a mark on a machine support, preserve that meaning in this scene. Check each placement in the assembled scene. Correct the particular mount or material called out in review. Inspect a close shot for mounting and a wide shot for the quote’s colour key.
- **Verify the operator that is actually drawn** (11-26). Compare its glove position with the product through pickup and release, watch the garment follow the shoulder and elbow, and check the table edge as the body reaches and turns. Run the same handling cycle on each supported line.
- **The bar:** serious, helpful and realistic; it explains the whole story and loads fast. Deliver only when it meets all of it, checked step by step.

## Two variants

Every line comes in up to two variants on the same engine, scene and realism (the pharma line is the bar for both; never flat grey shapes):

- **Catalogue**: the site's `/animations/<industry>-line` page. It autoplays as a loop with captions and the column, has no controls, and the site's header carries the logo. The rest of this file is written for it.
- **Explainer**: a page the viewer drives, for a proposal or to explain how a line works. On top of the catalogue rules:
  - **One control bar** at the bottom of the title-safe area, a white surface with a 1 px hairline (`#E2E8F0`), square corners and the guide's raised shadow, 100 px tall with its two rows (56 px with one). It is made of the design system's controls at their website form (square, 36 px): the primary button for play and pause, ghost buttons with the brand's arrow for back and forward 5 s, the timeline (a 4 px track, brand-blue progress, a 4 × 16 px bar for its thumb, a hairline at each chapter, the chapter's name in a 12 px tooltip on hover or focus), the time in tabular numerals, the camera views as one toggle group, and full screen. A second row holds the chapters and the speeds as text buttons, so the viewer navigates by name; chapter names on buttons are one to four words. Where the bar is too narrow for that row, the chapters and speeds move into one menu (Lucide sliders), then the views too; on a phone, play, the timeline and the menu. No row wraps. Space plays and pauses, the arrows seek.
  - **Chapters, not extra buttons.** A «how it works» moment is a chapter on the timeline.
  - **Labels are the overlay kit's label tabs** (20 px, 12 px Medium, ink on white with a 1 px hairline), one per subject, joined to it by a 1 px ink leader. No bullet dot before a label, no dot at a leader's end.
  - **The chapter's name** stands on the scene's clear top left, under the logo, in the overlay type scale (24 px Medium, its one line 16 px secondary), never on a card.
  - **The logo** sits on the scene in its clear space at the top left, as the guide sets it, with no box and no shadow.
  - **No circles that the scene doesn't have**: no circled icons, round buttons or round thumbs.
  - **The scene fills the window**: no letterbox bands.
  - **Product brands may appear.** Customer names appear only on a private link.
  - **One bar for every player**: the design system's `PlayerBar` block (`@aisgroup/design`, with the Slider's `timeline` form), never a piece's own.
  - **Auto first**: the film's own camera is the first view and the default. Held views (for example Wide, Station, Reject) hold the scene's own poses while the line runs, between the opening title and the results; the titles and the push into the software keep the film's framing.
  - **The chapter's name only where the top left is clear**, usually the wide view only, never on a phone while the subject is raised over the panel. Elsewhere the captions speak and the timeline and the menu name the chapter. Its line says where it happens, never what the caption says.
  - **Labels only in held views**, never over the logo, the chapter's name or a title, and named as the chapter names the part.
  - **The logo takes the white file over a dark ground**, chosen by contrast from the frame under it, read without stalling the GPU.
  - **The time as two numbers** either side of the timeline, elapsed and total, never «0:35 / 2:52».
  - **The bar takes its room**: captions, the column's content and the filmed screen stand above it (56 px plus 16 px clear).
  - **No stall on reveal**: render one warm-up frame of the scene alone before showing it, so the first frame's shader compile happens out of sight.
  - **A demo with its own clock is the bar's one action, not a chapter**: a «how it works» part that runs on its own clock (an ejector shown cycle by cycle, not a span of the film) opens from one design-system Button in PlayerBar's `actions` slot (a menu item under 768 px). In it the same bar drives the demo: its steps are the chapters, each step's words are the caption, play at the end replays, «Back to the film» (the brand's arrow) or Esc returns to the same moment. A moment of the film stays a chapter.
  - **Labels on the film's beats** where the original named its parts in the film: they show in Auto with their chapter, not only in held views; over the part, under it where that is taken, or on a leader twice as long where parts stand close; never over the logo, a title, a caption, the evidence, a mark or the bar.
  - **Claims and figures in the captions** where the original put them on chapter cards: the chapter's name and its line say what and where, the caption says the claim with every figure. «≈» becomes «about» where the font's subset lacks the glyph.
  - **The flagged product is marked by the overlay, as the Monitor marks a defect**: the kit's 2 px defect-red frame over an 8% tint on a white casing, fitted to the product as the camera sees it, from the scan to the reject and only where the camera sees it (a ray against the line's opaque parts); the products the evidence shows carry their class on a solid red tab on the frame's first free corner. No glow or ring in the 3D.
  - **Evidence beside the scene, not cards**: each rejected product a white plate with a hairline at the right on the title-safe margin: the scene's own picture of the defect, the verdict chip (28 px, an x and «NOK»), the class and where it was («Lane 3, row 195»); no leader across the scene; three at most. On phones only the newest, under the logo, and the chapter's name yields to it.
  - **Every shot centred above the bar**: the picture shifts up by half the bar's room (a lens shift). Narrower than 16:9 the vertical field widens so a shot keeps its width, down to 0.9:1.
  - **The titles' frame is the film's own, zoomed out and shifted low right** (a lens shift eased in and out with the camera's glides), so the titles and the results stand on the light ground, never over the machine.
  - **The camera by hand goes back to the film's frame when the titles' span starts**, and a chapter button seeks and flies the camera into Auto from where it is.
  - **No dip at the end**: the explainer starts and ends on the same frame, stops at its end, and play there starts it from the top.
  - **A customer's plant and line number are customer names** on an open URL.
  - **The AIS logo at the AIS site header's height** (44 px, 36 px on phones) on AIS pieces. No JetBrains Mono on the page layer: claims, rows and lanes in Inter.

**2026-11-26, machine and operator review.** «theres a logo image there on a camera», «the ok tick icon on that white circle», «check the physics and positions». Require evidence for a physical manufacturer mark. Use the guide's verdict renderer. Trace both ends of cables, review the operator's contact and scale, and test the visible machine during its working cycle. Each repeated fault needs a reproducible check in the skill.

## Brand

- **The brand guide, nothing off theme.** No decorative edge bars, accent stripes, invented chips or tiles.
- **Explanations on white.** Explanatory plates are white with a 1 px hairline (`#e2e8f0`), square corners and no edge bar. Graphics sit on blue or on white, never both.
- **Captions are the guide's captions** (Video, Captions): Inter Medium, 40 px at 1080p (3.7% of the stage height), white on the ink plate `#0F1729`, each line its own square plate (padding 0.15 × 0.4 em, plates 6 px apart), centred at the bottom of the title-safe area. Two lines at most, 42 characters a line, broken by hand at natural phrases (never «Four / cameras»), 1 to 7 s each. A caption changes with the shot it describes and never runs over a cut to a new subject.
- **The guide's motion, in real seconds.** Graphics enter over 500 ms on `cubic-bezier(.16,1,.3,1)`, rising 16 px at 1080p and fading with the move, and leave over 180 ms on `cubic-bezier(.4,0,1,1)`. They never scale, rotate or blur. Titles use masked type: lines rise from behind a mask over 1.1 s, 3 frames apart. Verified figures count up over 1.1 s in tabular numerals. When the piece plays slower than real time, these durations stay in real seconds.
- **The guide's transitions.** Cuts by default; never a dip to black, never a dissolve between different scenes. Views inside the app change as cuts, as the app does. Into the software, the camera pushes in on the Panel PC and lands exactly on the framing the app is filmed at, in one continuous move with no wipe.
- **Titles stand on the scene, never on cards.** No plates, no full-frame colour cards, no logo end card: the site's header carries the brand. The opening title, the results and the closing title stand in ink on the scene's clear top left, over the whole line framed low and right of centre, the way a product page sets its hero. Title Inter Semibold 96 px at 1080p with its line in Medium 40 px secondary; results as counted figures (Medium 80 px, ink, never brand blue) in a 2 × 2 grid; the closing title Semibold 72 px in three masked lines with one sentence of what the piece showed. The piece starts and ends on the same pose, so the loop wraps on one shot: the closing title leaves and the opening title rises. Sizes in container units of the stage; phones break titles at phrases.
- **Type never jumps.** The opening title's words rise only once Inter has loaded (`document.fonts.load`, with a 2.5 s fallback): a rise in the fallback font followed by a swap reads as a jump.
- **Titles align with the header logo's left edge.** The opening title, the results and the closing title start at the logo's left edge (the site container's edge plus its gutter), and the opening shot frames the whole line large below and to the right, so no empty strip is left at the left. Check it with `align.mjs` at four widths.
- **Never hold a frame for a title that has gone.** The opening shot keeps the line low and right for the title; once the title leaves, the camera turns (a slow pan from the same place, no flight) to the whole line centred, holds it, then cuts to the Panel PC.
- **Evidence, not cards.** No card with a blue title, thumbnails and a checklist of icons; no stat tiles. Show what the system shows: the camera images with the model's boxes as the Monitor draws them, the codes against the order as a plain table, the laser profile as a Data-page chart, one verdict chip. No titles over them: the caption says what they show. A qualifier that keeps a claim true goes in the chart's title («Laser height profile on this line, mm»). The checks are one sentence, not an icon list.
- **One layout: the picture alone, or split with a column.** A split frame has the picture on the left and a full-height white column on the right (from 50% across beside the line, from 62% beside the software) holding what explains it. The column slides in and out (0.8 s, smootherstep) while the picture reframes into the part left of it: the subject pans to that part's centre, and the Panel PC's screen moves and scales to stay whole and centred in it. Its content fades in once it has landed and rides out with it at its landed width, never reflowing as it narrows. Two columns that meet at the same width stay; only their content changes (out 180 ms, in 500 ms). Captions centre under the picture's part.
- **Explanations fill their column,** laid out from the column's own size, never a small card centred in it: the four views in a row as the Monitor shows them, each cropped round its bottle, with the unwrap the whole width under them; the codes' crop and the table; the top camera beside its readings with the laser profile the whole width under them. Leave room above and below a box for its tab (22 px), never on the bottle.
- **Overlays on the guide's tokens, checked one by one.** Spacing on the 4 px base and 8 px rhythm (never 6, 10, 14, 18 or 22); the product type scale 12 (chart axes only), 14, 16, 20, 24; Semibold only for the film title and chart titles, Medium for headings; secondary text `#4B5563`. Charts as on the guide's Data page: gridlines `#E2E8F0`, zero line `#94A3B8`, data-1 `#3664B3`, the legend above the chart, axes 12 px. The overlay kit: verdict chip 28 px tall, label tabs 20 px at 12 px Medium, defect red `#DC2626` on white. Audit every overlay against these before a release.
- **No generated look.** No decorative icons, icon tiles or rows of chips. Only a check, a cross or an arrow beside a status or a value.
- **No redundancy.** An overlay never repeats what the software on screen or the caption already says. One explanation per moment.
- **Explanations sell, once, and can be read.** The caption says what it means for the plant (the risk avoided, the work saved); the column shows the evidence. The two never say the same thing, and a panel's heading never repeats the caption. Keep every original claim and figure, and hold every word on screen long enough to read (see «Rhythm by reading time»).
- The general brand rules still apply: «» quotes, no «·» separators, no gradients, no glow, and never the words render, CAD or 3D in visible text.

## The software on screen

- **It is ours, copied and measured.** The panels are copied from the real software, measured, not imitated. The Monitor is arranged as on the real line, matching the published customer-story screenshot: the batch column, a wide live view with the four side views in one row and the unwrapped label under them (with the model's boxes, the cap box tight on the cap), Detections as a vertical column of thumbnails, and class charts and results at the right.
- **The software whole, never cropped.** On wide stages the Panel PC's screen is always shown whole, centred in the picture's part with its bezel's width clear all round and the caption band free below. No camera eases into a part of the app, and the frame's edge cuts nothing. Phones keep the flat app, cropped only in its gutters.
- **The real login.** Copy the product's real login page: the brand-blue half with the white logo, «AI Visual Inspection Platform», «Login to access your management panel.», and the product's own animated background (dot grid, sweeping beam, lock-on brackets), ported as a pure function of time so any moment can be held.
- **No heading elements in the copied UI or the overlays.** The site's CSS restyles every h1 to h4 inside its content, which breaks the copied layout; use `<p>` with classes.
- **Beside the filmed software, a pane, not a plate.** The audit trail beside the app sits in the column as a split frame, never a white card over the stainless door. One column; the title with its note directly under it (never split to opposite edges); 24 px to the rows; no footer floating at the pane's bottom. Its title stands level with the top of the app's screen, values in a change line are body size like the rest of the row, and the captions centre under the app, never across the split.
- **The screen sits low enough to read as a screen.** The filmed screen keeps its bezel's height (6.6% of the screen) free above it, so the door shows over it and it never touches the top.
- **The software is filmed on its screen, not floated on white.** On wide stages every software moment keeps its layout, but the scene films the Panel PC's screen square on, exactly where the app sits: bezel, stainless door and handle round it, the line behind wherever the frame reaches past the cabinet. Square on, the screen's picture is only scaled, so the app is drawn with a 2D matrix and stays sharp. Panels beside a cropped part of the screen start at the crop's gutter. Phones keep the flat app: a screen in a cabinet is unreadable at that size.
- **Only what the Panel PC shows.** The Monitor runs alone on the Panel PC, without the side menu.
- **Dialogs centred on the app's screen.** Modal dialogs and notices centre on the app's whole screen, over its dimmed page, as the real app centres them. `align.mjs` measures every dialog's and notice's offset from the screen's centre at four widths; 0 px, locally and live, is the bar.
- **Never cut the software.** Show the page whole. Any crop ends in a gutter, never through a widget.
- **Animate it as it behaves.** No invented motion in the software: no gliding or zooming windows, no drawn mouse pointer on a touch screen, no sliding panels or rising text inside the app. Its view changes are cuts. Typing fills fields as typed; presses show on the control. Our own graphics around it follow the guide's motion.
- **Show the Monitor long enough to see how it rejects.**
- **The copy is measured, component by component.** Measure each component on the real dashboard (Playwright at 1280 × 800) and copy its values. For example: the notification overlay opaque white, 24 × 32 px padding, 12 px corners, text 16/24, 16 px to its 8 px bar, shown and hidden at once (a fade reads as translucent), placed between rows of the batch column, never across a field; the dialog 470 px wide, its grey header (16 px padding, title 16/28 Medium), body 20 px padding with 16 px between items, 40 px buttons 12 px apart; the select menu, chart headers (44 px band) and card gaps (16 px) likewise. In the copy, the real app's values win over the guide's grid; report its off-theme values to the product team instead of restyling them.
- **Documents the app generates look like documents.** No filled stat tiles, bars or mixed sizes: 40 px margins, one value column, 14/20 text, labels secondary and values ink, key figures plain and in ink, the brand's blue logo in the header, the status with its icon and word. Keep every field and figure. Filmed, the report and the shared folder are two windows on the Panel PC's screen at one scale, 32 app px in from its edges, top-aligned, 24 px apart, never over the bezel or the door. The app opens them in place and whole: a fade, no rise, no sections arriving one by one. Long file names break at their underscores, never mid-word.

## The 3D

- **Realistic and physically right.** Every object moves as it would: nothing passes through anything, nothing floats, every part rests on something.
- **Real components, as installed.** Model the real parts from photos of the installation: a stack light on its pole and base, lit coloured buttons, cables that run in conduits and enter through glands.
- **Materials that look real.** Brushed stainless with grain and anisotropy under a neutral hall light, matched side by side to a real photo. The grain is also a bump map, strong enough to read in close-ups: compare A/B crops at the closest shots (the head, the cabinet door) and check that the wide shot stays clean.
- **Products fit the machine's openings.** Ray-cast every opening a product passes through against the product's real size. Keep the product at its real measured size and fit the format part to it; never scale the product down to suit a drawing made for another format.
- **Camera images look captured, not rendered.** Match the real Monitor's pictures: the chamber behind the bottle as the camera sees it, the chain's slats under it, the light ring all round so all four views are lit alike, the product's real glass. Then give the views the sensor's character on their own renderer: a cool white balance, lifted blacks, lower contrast, a touch of softness, lens fall-off and fine noise, all subtle. Each capture costs 30 to 70 ms: make them ahead, while the software is on screen and the line isn't drawn, never during a camera move.
- **Boxes fit what they name.** A box is the outline of what it names as that view sees it: the label's box runs to the label's visible edge, sampled densely round the product up to its silhouette, never a coarse grid of points that falls short. Check each view's box against the picture at full size.
- **Defects as a real line makes them.** Bound each defect by the physics of the pack: a 38-400 cap cocked on its thread pivots round the liner's edge and reaches at most atan(4.2 / 38.1) ≈ 6.3°, so the leaning cap is 6°, not a tilt no cap can take. Wrinkles are creases in the label's relief, shaded by the dome's light, never drawn lines; bubbles are low blisters that catch the light. Light the label as the dome does, so the defect reads without its box, and draw the model's boxes only from the camera views that see the defect.
- **Never slice the machine open.** No section planes. To show the inside, the camera goes where a small camera could.
- **Cameras in their windows.** Measure each camera window by ray-casting the model, centre the lens in it, and put the virtual cameras that make the camera images at the same place.
- **No diagram geometry in the scene.** Lines of sight, beams, fills and laser lines do not belong in the 3D, and no laser line is drawn on the products; the panels and captions explain.
- **The reject is an expulsion.** The paddle is a pneumatic kicker: it fires into the product mid-stroke (a stroke of about 60 ms), the product leaves the chain at about 1 m/s, flies the drop and slides to rest about 70% across the tray, clear of its walls, then wobbles out. Choose the firing moment by simulating every candidate: neighbours never touched, rest position scored. Show it in one held shot where the strike, the flight and the landing are all visible, from the downstream end of the line, not from behind the actuator's housing.
- **Physics checks, every time:** ray-cast openings against the products that pass through them, width and height (a product half through a slot is rightly hidden by the wall); check the paddle against every product over the whole loop; trace the kicked product (speed, landing, rest, room to the walls).
- **A conveyor that looks like one.** Compare it with a photo of the real line at the same framing: the chain's links and hinges, its sheen, the side channel's thickness and folds, the rails' brackets, the legs' feet and cross braces.
- **Cameras well inside what they film.** A camera closer to a wall than its near plane (10 mm) draws the wall as stripes. Keep every camera more than the near plane from any wall, and check every inside shot with `pick.mjs` on the odd pixels, not by eye on one frame.
- **Clean camera ports.** Each window gets a dark gasket bent to the wall and a black tunnel behind it, so the port reads as a machined opening with the lens inside it.

- **A hall, not a set.** Lines stand on the reference hall: a light grey epoxy floor poured in 2 m bays with saw-cut joints, yellow walkway tape round each line, air that fades into the page's colour, a hall HDRI for the steel to reflect. No stand-in machines, ceiling strips or walls: flat blocks and bright strips read as fake and streak across the wide shots. Every line in shot casts its shadow; on a light floor a line without one floats (check it in a top-down frame: the sun throws shadows away from the film's camera).
- **Canvas textures on the CPU.** An accelerated 2D canvas can lose its first draw before the GPU uploads it (a floor drew black, screens stayed black until their first redraw). Make every canvas that becomes a texture with `getContext('2d', { willReadFrequently: true })`, and read a pixel back when a surface looks wrong.
- **A screen in the 3D is the Monitor copy.** An HMI or Panel PC that is never filmed square-on draws the Monitor's measured layout on a canvas at its 1280 × 800 design size (batch column, live view with the model's boxes, Detections from the scene's own pictures, class charts, results), in Inter and the Monitor's colours, its Lucide icons drawn as paths (the Inter subset has no ✓, ✕ or ■); never an invented screen or a title bar with «·». It redraws on a quantised story clock (twice a story second) and at the size it is seen (half on background stations): redrawn at every product it cost 30 ms frames.
- **Logos on the machine are the guide's files**, at their own aspect and in their clear space (a cut-out, a nameplate, a badge); never the name typed in a box.
- **Anisotropy only along the part's UVs.** Brushed anisotropy needs UVs that run along the grain; rods and brackets with box-projected UVs, or none, draw black and white streaks. Give them an anodised finish without anisotropy.
- **Weight where nobody sees it.** Measure triangles, draw calls and frame times on the real GPU with the world alone at fixed poses, before and after a change. Thousands of instanced products lose the faces that are always covered (inner discs, the underside on the belt, a lathe's zero-area triangles), and background lines draw their rejects through the same instances, not a group per product.

## Camera, framing and pace

- **Never too fast.** Give every beat time to be read.
- **No camera flights.** Held shots with a slow drift, joined by cuts.
- **No jumps between the scene and the screens.** The camera pushes in on the Panel PC to the exact square-on pose the software is filmed at, so the move ends where the software shot begins.
- **The opening is the title, then the whole line, centred,** held about 2 s after the title lifts off, before the first move.
- **Every view framed for its subject.** With the column open, the scene frames its subject in the middle of the part left of it, never behind the column. A subject that crosses the frame fast gets a tracking shot: follow it down the line at its height, then cut on its action to the next view so it is still in shot when the verdict lands.
- **Rhythm by reading time.** Captions read at 15 to 18 characters a second (BBC 160 to 180 words a minute; Netflix 5/6 s to 7 s an event). What gives time to read is the stable hold after an entrance, not the layer's life, and a cut while someone is still reading is the classic fault. So every caption and every column's content holds, settled, at least its characters ÷ 15 s plus 1 s to find it (a picture or a chart 1.5 s more), and nothing cuts away before. The piece checks this in development. The beat sheet stays; the clock's rate varies.
- **A speed ramp holds the measured moment.** Product films hold their key physical moment with a speed ramp, not a cut: ease into slow motion over about 0.5 to 1 s, hold, ease back out. The line is back at speed before any physics that must play at real speed (the reject). Never a freeze, never a cut to slow motion; a strobe stays a flash in real time.
- **Transitions in sync.** The caption, the column's content and the cut change together, on the cut: never a blank column between two contents, never three changes at three moments.
- **Professional shots.** One subject a shot, whole and clear: nothing big and out of focus cut by the frame's edge, no rail across the subject, no half a machine at the edge. Outside, a long lens (fov 20 to 30°) at the subject's height, the line leading into the frame; inside the machine, a symmetric look along the line, the lenses in their windows balanced on both sides. Moves are slow and motivated: a push towards what comes next, a track with the subject. A drift that shows nothing new is cut. Check a tracking shot over its whole span: a track that starts after its subject has gone is a still frame the subject slides out of.
- **Cut into the machine on the action.** A match cut: outside, the product half through the slot; inside, the same product at the same moment coming through it. A housing crossing the lens can serve as a foreground wipe that hides the cut. One shot shows the whole system, such as the four lenses in their ports round the product from overhead (no roll). A reading worth two angles gets two, so the second is a new image, not the first held longer.
- **Follow what travels.** After the verdict, a signal running to the cabinet motivates the move; it runs slowly enough (about 1 s) to be followed. No static wide where the action is a small light far away.
- **No compound moves.** One motion at a time: never a pan while the frame also re-centres for the column. Where the action travels across the frame (a signal along a cable), hold a locked-off frame that contains its whole path and let the eye follow it.
- **No worm's-eye views, no jump in scale.** A camera below the subject looking up reads as a mistake in a product film; inside the machine the camera stands at the subject's height or a little above, never under it. Going in, the scale steps down (wide, medium, close), or the cut matches the framing on both sides; never from a wide on the line straight to an extreme close-up of a part in profile.
- **The panel never contradicts the picture.** A panel about the product in shot shows that product; before it is measured the values wait (a dash, no trace) and they land when it passes the measurement.
- **Rejections: the Monitor beside the defect, close up.** While the Monitor rejects, the column shows each rejected product as the camera saw it, close up (rendered from the view that sees the defect best, the model's box on it), its verdict and name, one sentence of what it is, and the count by cause with the cause just counted marked; a good product shows its four views clean.
- **The subject is always in shot inside.** Products pass at the line's pitch, so a camera looking across the line sees an empty chamber between them. Look along the line instead, with the products coming towards the camera and passing under it; check the beat at 1 s steps, not on one frame.
- **Out of the software, the push reversed.** Into the software the camera pushes in to the screen; out of it, it pulls back to where the push started, then cuts. At the end, one continuous pull from the screen out to the whole line, where the results and the closing title stand. Never a cut straight from the app to another subject.
- **Show where the camera goes before it goes there.** Before a cut inside the machine, a slow push on its infeed with the products going in, so the inside reads as following the product.
- **Never project what is behind the camera.** Draw a plane (the Panel PC's screen) only when all its corners are in front of the camera; corners behind it project mirrored.
- **Frame what is explained.** The subject is whole and clear of the captions and panels; nothing important sits under a caption.

## Stage, phones and full screen

- **The page never collapses.** The piece fits the viewport under the site header (the stage is `100dvh` minus the header's height), and every framing is judged on that stage, not the window. Overlays stay hidden until the scene is live; a lost GPU context falls back to the poster.
- **Phones get their own framing.** A portrait stage keeps the lens's vertical field, so a landscape shot is cropped to its middle, and the panel covers the lower 40%. While the panel is up the subject sits in the middle of the room above it, and a close-up the narrow frame would crop declares a wider field for phones. Film the key moments at 390 × 844 every round.
- **Playable full screen.** A 40 px icon button (Lucide maximize / minimize, white with a hairline) in the top right corner, clear of the titles and the captions, puts the stage itself in full screen; the scene and the UI layer follow its size. Hide it where the browser can't (`document.fullscreenEnabled`, iPhone).

## Loading

- **Loads fast, measured.** Measure the page's transfer and its time to live, by file, locally and on the live site; then cut the biggest first. Keep assets small and compare old and new at the same moments before swapping: a light map at 256 × 128 is indistinguishable on brushed steel, and a machine simplified to about 0.4 M triangles is indistinguishable at the closest framings. Cull the triangles no shot ever sees (`cull.mjs`, verified frame by frame against the original).
- **Preload what the scene needs.** Preload the big files from the page (`<link rel="preload" as="fetch" crossorigin>`, allowed in the body) so they start with the page, not after the scripts. Labels load with the page.
- **Nothing heavy on the way to live.** Build a procedural fallback environment only if the light map fails, never synchronously at boot. Disable local clipping the piece doesn't use. Make the camera images in a WebGL2 `OffscreenCanvas` worker, so going live never waits for hundreds of GPU readbacks.
- **Resize light maps in float.** Some ImageMagick builds clamp HDR at 1 and darken every reflection: box-filter the RGBE data in float instead.
- **The first frame is the opening title, in the page's own markup.** The title is HTML and CSS in the page over the poster: it paints with the page, its words rise by CSS before any script (once the font is ready), and it holds while the model and the light load. Once the scene is live, its clock starts where the CSS entrance has got to (the animation's start time against the document timeline), holding the title at least 1 s more, so nothing jumps or restarts. Trace a real load (word positions every 60 ms, `loadtrace.mjs`) to prove it.
- **The clock never runs backwards or leaps.** After long tasks, such as shaders compiling at load, the first frame's timestamp can lag the clock's start by most of a second, and a negative step wraps the story to its end. Clamp each step to 0 to 0.1 s.
- **The poster is the current first frame.** The opening title is HTML over the poster while the scene loads, so the poster must be the scene's own first frame (t = 0, the UI layer on, the title off), very wide (2700 × 1000) with the scene's view offset, placed with `object-position` at that offset so the handover to the live scene doesn't move. With reduced motion, without WebGL or after a failed load, the poster is the piece. Render it again whenever the scene changes.

## Process

- Check every change at full size, frame by frame, before showing it; compare it with the original at the same moments and with the real software.
- Write every review remark down as a rule before fixing it.
- Run every capture and build through `scripts/safe.sh`: WebGL captures can exhaust a machine's memory, and the script runs each job in a capped scope that the kernel stops first. One Chromium at a time.
- Make every code edit in the website's working repository. When a separate release checkout exists, copy into it only as the final release step, never edit both while parallel agents run.
